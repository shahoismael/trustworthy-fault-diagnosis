## a b s t r a c t

In this work, we present a physics-informed recurrent neural network (PIRNN) modeling approach, and a PIRNN-based predictive control scheme for a general class of nonlinear dynamic systems. Specifically, we first develop a hybrid data-driven and physics-guided modeling framework that integrates measurement data and mechanistic mathematical models to construct high-fidelity RNN models. Then, we derive a generalization error bound of the PIRNN model based on a nominal system model via the Rademacher complexity technique from statistical machine learning theory. Subsequently, the PIRNN model is utilized in Lyapunov-based model predictive controllers and applied to a chemical reactor example with Gaussian measurement noise to demonstrate its improved noise rejection and generalization performance in comparison to the purely data-driven and the purely physics-guided RNN-based predictive control schemes.

## 1. Introduction

Model predictive control (MPC) is an optimization-based advanced control method that solves for the optimal control actions based on a process model while accounting for the intrinsic dynamic behavior of the process. It is a highly flexible and powerful control scheme that permits the inclusion of practical constraints (e.g., temperature limits of a heating jacket) on the manipulated variables, which facilitates the search of feasible solutions to optimization-based control problems by respecting any physical limits imposed. However, a major challenge that prohibits the real-time implementation of MPC to nonlinear processes has been largely associated with model representation and identification. The development of accurate and computationally efficient process models thus remains the bottleneck. In attempts to address this limitation, one of the promising pursuits encompasses the synergy between machine learning (ML) and MPC, which promotes the conversion of process data into manufacturing intelligence via the integration between data-driven modeling and model-based control methods. Specifically, ML-MPC has achieved astounding computational and control performances in previous works (e.g., in controlling a diffusion-reaction process [1], a batch crystallization process [2], and a plasma etch process [3]).

However, although ML-MPC seems to be the hallmark among the state-of-the-art industrial process control techniques, a major concern associated with this approach is the generalization capability of the ML models, which is contingent on the quality of training data [4]. Limited or inadequately sampled training data can compromise the predictive prowess of ML models, and this greatly hinders the prescriptive performance and hence the applicability of ML-MPC to real-world manufacturing systems. To this end, as standard ML modeling techniques generally require an extensive set of training data, which may not always be readily available or is cumbersome to obtain, to warrant satisfactory predictive performance, more practicable alternatives are thus of compelling need. To address this issue, many works have been developed to embed prior physical knowledge into the formulation of neural networks (e.g., Refs. [5–9]). For example, in Ref. [9], a priori process structural knowledge was incorporated into the design of RNN architecture, and demonstrated its superiority in improving approximation performance compared to a conventional fully-connected RNN model. Additionally, the physics-informed neural network (PINN) modeling approach, which was initially proposed in Ref. [10], has elicited a substantial interest in the modeling industry [11]. Fundamentally, PINN effectively overcomes the small data problem by informatively exploiting a priori knowledge of the nonlinear system for constructing robust feedforward neural networks. In the physicsinformed modeling framework, training of the neural network is facilitated by directly embedding the underlying governing physical laws (e.g., physical phenomena expressed in terms of ordinary differential equations (ODE) or partial differential equations (PDE)) as regularization terms (e.g., nonlinear differential operators on the outputs of the model) in the cost function of the learning problem [10,11]. Intuitively and functionally, the addition of the penalty terms augments the learning process by enforcing the required behavior imposed not only by empirical data, but also by the mechanistic laws. The regularization effect is thus instrumental to the neural network in conforming to the a priori structure dictated by the physical laws, and this moderates the reliance on exhaustively large process datasets [4,12].

Subsequently, a number of extensions and variants to the original PINN modeling approach have since been proposed: parareal PINN (PPINN) for parallelized learning of very longtime integration of a time-dependent PDE via decomposing the long-time problem into several distinct short-time problems [13], multi-fidelity PINN (MPINN) that learns from training data of disparate precisions [14,15], Bayesian PINN (BPINN) for solving both the forward and inverse nonlinear problems involving noisy data [16], and DeepXDE that incorporates a residual-based adaptive refinement (RAR) technique for boosting the training efficiency of PINN [17]. In addition, the applications of PINN are expansive and span across numerous engineering disciplines. Previous works have explored PINN for various applications, such as in modeling of exothermic heat transfer in a composite-tool system [18], identification of unknown geometric and material parameters for characterizing internal structures and defects in materials [19], and predicting fluid flow in the gas drainage of a water-filled porous medium [20]. However, while the physicsinformed learning methodology has been widely applied to feedforward neural networks [10], its applicability to other notable neural network architectures (e.g., recurrent neural network (RNN) that has been widely used to model nonlinear dynamic systems) remains to be investigated. Furthermore, theoretical analysis of the generalization performance that characterizes the model’s ability to sufficiently adapt to previously unseen data of the same distribution has not been reported, and the application of physics-based machine learning modeling in advanced control methods (e.g., MPC) is still in its infancy [12].

Motivated by the above considerations, in this manuscript, we develop a general physics-based modeling framework for constructing RNN models and formulating a physics-informed RNN (PIRNN)-based predictive control scheme for nonlinear systems. Specifically, we first present a hybrid modeling approach (i.e., PIRNN) that integrates data-driven and physics-informed techniques, following the framework outlined in Ref. [10]. Next, we develop the analysis of the generalization performance of PIRNN on the nominal nonlinear process model, and incorporate the PIRNN model into the MPC scheme. Finally, the effectiveness of the PIRNN-MPC scheme is demonstrated through a continuous stirred tank reactor (CSTR) example with Gaussian measurement noise, where the closed-loop performance of the PIRNN-MPC is compared with that of the first-principles model-, a purely datadriven RNN model-, and a purely physics-based RNN model-MPC control schemes.

## 3. Physics-informed (PI) RNN

In this section, a synergistic way of integrating and embedding mechanistic knowledge into the neural network’s learning (i.e., physics-informed (PI) learning), as proposed in Ref. [10], is introduced. The PI learning strategy integrates the information from both actual measurements and physical laws (e.g., process dynamics governed by Eq. (1a)) by incorporating the governing ODEs as additional penalty terms into the loss function of an RNN. Consequently, the introduction of physics-based penalty terms imposes a priori structure dictated by the physical laws on the RNN, which improves the extrapolating capability of the model (e.g., predicting system dynamics beyond the range of training data) and the model generalization performance.

## 3.1. Recurrent neural network (RNN) model

In contrast to the one-way connectivity between neurons in a conventional feedforward NN, signals in recurrent neural networks (RNN) travel in bi-directions (i.e., forward and backward) due to the introduction of recurrent connections (i.e., loops) in the network architecture. This permits the feedback of signals derived from previous inputs back into the network, thereby exhibiting a temporal dynamic behavior which inherently resembles the numerical procedures (e.g., the explicit Euler method) for solving an ODE. Therefore, the architecture of the RNN is especially apposite for modeling nonlinear continuous systems governed by ODEs. Furthermore, as a powerful dynamic modeling approach, the application of RNN has achieved remarkable results across a wide range of disciplines (e.g., natural language processing [22], speech recognition [23], computational biology [24], and general time-series forecasting involving sequential data [25]).

In this work, a recurrent neural network (RNN) model that approximates the nonlinear dynamics of the system of Eq. (1) is developed, which can be represented by the following form:

Traditionally, the development of an RNN model for the continuous-time nonlinear system employs a three-step approach:

1. A comprehensively large dataset consisting of state trajectories from diverse initial conditions in the stable operating region $( { \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } } x _ { 0 } \in \Omega _ { \rho } )$ and control actions spanning their physical operating limits $( \mathrm { i } . \mathsf { e } . , u \in U )$ is collected through conducting extensive experimental runs of the nonlinear system of Eq. 2. A machine learning framework (e.g., Tensorflow and Py-Torch) is utilized to develop and train an RNN to obtain the optimal weights (i.e., the coefficient matrices A and Ψ ) by minimizing the loss function that encompasses the mean squared error (MSE) between the predicted and actual system states from the RNN and the sensor measurement, respectively.

3. The predictive performance of the trained RNN model will be evaluated using a test dataset that has been intentionally excluded from the training process, where the model outputs will be compared with the actual state trajectories acquired.

However, although the universal approximation theorem states that an RNN with a suitable architecture is capable of approximating any nonlinear dynamic system to an arbitrary accuracy on compact subsets of the state space for finite time [26], algorithmic learnability of the optimal neural network weights is not guaranteed. In reality, pinpointing the optimal weights and model hyperparameters (e.g., number of layers and recurrent units) for the regression tasks of a large-scale and complex nonlinear system is a multifaceted problem, which is often demanding due to the involvement of various factors such as the complexity of neural network structure, availability of computing resources, and feasibility of optimization algorithms. Therefore, improving the performance of neural networks has been a long-standing challenge for researchers in machine learning community over the past few decades, where significant efforts have been made to optimize neural network architecture, develop advanced optimization algorithms, and improve data quality (e.g., via design of experiments).

In this work, we will improve the performance of RNN models by incorporating domain knowledge of the nonlinear system into the RNN development, as proposed in Ref. [10]. The formulation of physics-informed (PI) RNN will be presented in the next subsection.

## 3.2. Regularization through physics-guided loss function

Physics-guided learning effectively harnesses mechanistic knowledge encapsulated in process data and mathematical models for building accurate and robust surrogate RNN models. The incorporation of the physical-law-based regularization terms as soft constraints in the loss function furnishes an RNN with a priori knowledge by reasonably and informatively restricting the weights of the neural network, which in turn reinforces the learning and identification of solutions that are consistent with the physics-based model. The physics-informed learning can often be viewed as a subset of multi-task learning where the RNN model is concurrently enforced to fit the observed data (e.g., data from sensors), and to generate outputs that conform to the physics-based constraints (e.g., governing equation of Eq. (1a)). To simplify the discussion, we assume that the full process model of Eq. (1a) is known for the design of physics-informed RNNs in this section. However, it should be clarified that the proposed PIRNN modeling approach does not necessarily require the knowledge of a full process model. In other words, the proposed PIRNN modeling approach can be applied when the first-principles model is partially known. For example, consider a real-world system where data is only available for a certain number of system states that can be easily measured. We can take advantage of the first-principles model that describes the unmeasured states (i.e., partial physical knowledge), and incorporate it into physicsinformed RNNs, together with training data for measured states. In this way, the resulting RNN model with partial knowledge of the first-principles model and limited training data will fit well on the provided training data points, and is also able to predict the dynamics of the unmeasured states based on prior physical knowledge. The integration of (partial) physical knowledge with the process data available improves the applicability of ML modeling approaches and the generalization performance over a wider range of operating conditions.

To put it formally, a new function G is first defined to be the equivalent of the left-hand side of Eq. (1a).

$$
\mathcal {G} (\tilde {x}, u) := \dot {\tilde {x}} - F (\tilde {x}, u)\tag{4}
$$

where $\tilde { x }$ represents the output of an RNN, which accepts the initial state vector $x _ { 0 }$ of the dynamic system, and the manipulated input u as its inputs. $\tilde { x } = h ( z ; \theta ) , z = [ x _ { 0 } ,$ , u] denotes the RNN input vector, and $h ( z ; \theta )$ is the approximation of the solution x of the nonlinear system of Eq. (1a) for time $0 \leq t \leq \varDelta$ subject to the same $x _ { 0 }$ and u. Consequently, as the RNN learns to compute the solution x of the ODE governed by Eq. (1a), the following expression holds.

$$
\mathcal {G} (\tilde {x}, u) := \dot {\tilde {x}} - F (\tilde {x}, u) \approx 0, t \in [ 0, \Delta ]\tag{5}
$$

Specifically, the PIRNN $h ( z ; \theta )$ is trained using an optimizer such as Adam and two groups of data, which correspond to their respective penalty terms presented in the mean squared error (MSE) loss function (Eq. (6)). The first group of data is involved in the computation of $M S E _ { \mathcal { X } }$ of Eq. (6), and consists of the process data captured by sensors from experiments or past operations that resemble the training data used in a typical regression problem. The second group of data pertains to $M S E _ { \mathcal { G } }$ and comprises only the collocation points of the initial state vector and manipulated inputs that are randomly and uniformly sampled from the designated operating regions $( \varOmega _ { \rho } )$ where the RNN approximation (Eq. (5)) should be valid.

$$

The superscript (i) in $t ^ { ( i ) }$ is the time index. It should be noted that the training data garnered for calculating the first loss term $M S E _ { \mathcal { X } }$ are directly captured from the actual nonlinear system near a set of desired setpoints, and the collocation points are thus sampled predominantly from operating regions that are beyond the range of training data.

Consequently, the two loss terms $M S E _ { \mathcal { X } }$ and $M S E _ { \mathcal { G } }$ collectively aim to enforce the dynamic behavior that conforms to the physical laws (e.g., mass and energy balances) while contemporaneously forcing the best possible fit to plain training data points, and this improves the interpretability of PIRNNs in the operating region with no data available. It should also be noted that, as denoted in Eq. $( 7 )  { - } ( 8 )$ , the output of the PIRNN model consists of the system states from $t = t _ { 0 }$ onward (instead of starting from $t = t _ { 1 } )$ , and this was found to facilitate the learning process especially for minimizing the physics-loss $M S E _ { \mathcal G } .$ In addition, when the PIRNN is trained solely by minimizing the loss function of Eq. (7), the training process resembles the standard learning algorithm that seeks minimize the MSE difference between the predicted and the labeled output data. On the other extreme end where no labeled data is available, the PIRNN model can be trained entirely based on Eq. (8) only, for which the model will eventually conform to the dynamic behavior dictated by the governing ODE at the given initial conditions.

Remark 2. In case the process model $F ( x , u )$ is not fully known or involves unknown parameters, the inverse problem of physicsinformed machine learning has been recently developed to learn unknown parameters from data $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ ., Ref. [10,16,27]). Specifically, a new function G(x˜, $u , \lambda ) : = \dot { \tilde { x } } - F ( \tilde { x } , u , \lambda )$ , similar to Eq. (4), can be developed in the inverse problem to learn unknown parameters λ in the incomplete physics model. The inverse problem of physicsinformed machine learning is essentially a multi-task learning where the neural network model is trained to not only achieve the best possible fit to the observed data, but also estimate the values of λ that conform to the physical laws. After λ is identified, we can then carry out the forward problem proposed in this manuscript to generate sufficient collocation points from $F ( \tilde { x } , u , \lambda )$ to learn the dynamics of the given system. Additionally, in case the state functions are unknown for some states in F (x, u), physics-informed machine learning method can include the state functions that are fully known only, since these are the physical knowledge we have.

Remark 3. The proposed physics-informed machine learning method provides a solution to improving model performance in the presence of insufficient data with the help of physical knowledge. In addition to improved generalization performance with the use of physical knowledge, physics-informed NNs can potentially improve the computational efficiency compared to first-principles model (if known) due to the nature of the matrix operations involved in NNs. It has been demonstrated in many recent works that NNs can significantly reduce the computational cost for solving nonlinear dynamic systems $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ , Ref. [2,28–31]). Furthermore, another benefit of conventional NNs or physicsinformed NNs is their ability to model a wide variety of functions and the possibility to reduce the time and cost associated with physical model’s design, tuning, and adaptation to new plant’s operating conditions (see more discussions in Ref. [32]).

## 3.3. Physics-informed RNN training

The PIRNN model is developed using PyTorch, an open-source machine learning framework based on the Torch library [33]. The inputs to the PIRNN model consist of the system initial state vector $x _ { 0 } ,$ the manipulated input u, and the (optional) times t at which the future system states are to be predicted (i.e., as outputs of the model). In this work, the nonlinear dynamic system is assumed to be predominantly operating at a specific steady-state setpoint, and hence the process data collected only capture the system dynamics in a small neighborhood around the setpoint. Note that this assumption is made to better demonstrate the improved generalization performance of PIRNNs in the entire operating region in the absence of sufficient data, compared to conventional data-driven modeling approaches. It should also be clarified that the limited data assumption made in this section is not a requirement for the implementation of the proposed PIRNN approach. It is used to showcase the benefits of PIRNN over purely data-driven modeling approaches, as the latter approaches can certainly achieve a sufficiently small modeling error when data is exhaustive (i.e., the process data is available for the entire operating region).

In this work, the process data is developed by numerically solving the nonlinear dynamic system of Eq. (1a) using the Explicit Euler method with an integration time step of $h _ { c }$ , and with initial states $x _ { 0 }$ and manipulated inputs u randomly sampled in a small region around their respective steady-state setpoints. These state measurements are subject to the measurement noise of Eq. (1b) to mimic data retrieved from real-life process operations. The collocation points are developed by randomly and uniformly sampling $x _ { 0 }$ and u beyond the setpoints and across the entire stability region $\varOmega _ { \rho } ,$ and the physical limits of the manipulated inputs $U ,$ respectively. Specifically, no labeled data is required for training using the collocation points. Instead, the ODE is explicitly embedded in the loss function of the RNN in the way that the LHS of the ODE $( \dot { x } = F ( x , u ) )$ is calculated based on the RNN predicted states, where the derivative is approximated using the finite difference method. The RHS of the ODE (i.e., the function $F ( x , u ) )$ is computed by substituting the PIRNN predicted states and the manipulated inputs into $F ( x , u )$ . The second loss term $M S E _ { \mathcal { G } }$ in the loss function of Eq. (8) is to minimize the error between the LHS and RHS of the ODE using the PIRNN prediction results so that the PIRNN can learn the underlying physical principles. Therefore, in principle, numerical solution to the ODE is not required for the proposed PIRNN approach, which can significantly reduce the computation time for training a PIRNN.

In addition, scaling factors w and $w _ { \mathscr { G } }$ are incorporated into the total loss function (i.e., $M S E = w _ { \mathcal { X } } \cdot M S E _ { \mathcal { X } } + w _ { \mathcal { G } } \cdot M S E _ { \mathcal { G } } )$ to ensure both MSE loss terms are of approximately equal scale. Specifically, while $M S E _ { \mathcal { X } }$ is in exact analogous to the conventional MSE loss employed in a typical ML regression task, the calculation of $M S E _ { \mathcal G }$ demands the determination of the both the derivative of $\bar { \tilde { x } } \ ( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ \dot { \tilde { x } } )$ and the standard MSE between the predicted states at $t ~ = ~ t _ { 0 }$ and the initial states supplied to the PIRNN (i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { 1 } { N _ { C } } \sum _ { n = 1 } ^ { N _ { C } } | x _ { n } ( t _ { \mathcal { G } } ^ { ( 0 ) } , u _ { \mathcal { G } , n } ) - \tilde { x } _ { n } ( t _ { \mathcal { G } } ^ { ( 0 ) } , u _ { \mathcal { G } , n } ) | ^ { 2 } ) . } \end{array}$ . As shown in Fig. 1, $\dot { \tilde { x } }$ can be approximated by the finite difference method using the predicted outputs from the PIRNN directly. $h _ { c }$ is the integration time step (i.e., time interval between two successive time steps).

## 4. Generalization error analysis for physics-informed RNN

In this section, we will evaluate the generalization performance of the PIRNN model of Eqs. (3)–(5) by developing a generalization error bound using statistical learning theory. In our previous work, a generalization error bound for purely datadriven RNN models with one loss term (i.e., the standard MSE loss of the training data) in the loss function was derived in Ref. [38]. With respect to physics-informed NNs, the generalization error bounds for physics-informed feedforward NNs approximating solutions of the forward and inverse problems for PDEs were derived in Refs. [39,40], respectively. However, the generalization error bounds in Refs. [39,40] were derived for a general class of data-driven models that do not account for the architecture of NNs, and therefore, the generalization error results cannot be used to guide the construction of NNs in terms of the number of neurons and layers, etc. In this section, we derive – for the first time – a generalization error bound for physics-informed RNN models that account for the physics-based regularization term in loss function, and the architecture of RNNs, where the proof technique of Rademacher complexity for RNNs follows Ref. [38]. Specifically, we consider the extreme scenario where no historical data is available, and only the initial conditions $x _ { 0 }$ and the firstprinciples model of Eq. (1a) are used to develop the PIRNN model In other words, the PIRNN will be trained using the loss function of Eq. (8) only to fit the initial condition x and capture the nonlinear dynamics of Eq. (1a). Let $\tilde { x } = h ( z ; \theta )$ denote the predicted output of the PIRNN model, where $z = [ x _ { 0 }$ u] is the PIRNN input and θ is a set of all the PIRNN weight matrices. The generalization error for the PIRNN model $h ( z , \theta )$ , with z drawn independently from some underlying distribution $z \in Z$ , is defined as follows, where $Z$ is the set of bounded states $x _ { 0 } ~ \in ~ \varOmega _ { \rho }$ and bounded manipulated inputs u $\in U .$

$$
R _ {D} (\theta) := \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {z} \sim Z} [ L (\mathcal {G} (h (z; \theta), u), 0) ] + \gamma \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {z} \sim Z} [ L (\tilde {x} _ {0}, x _ {0}) ]\tag{9}
$$

$L \left( \cdot , \cdot \right)$ denotes the loss function, where the MSE loss is used in this study. $\gamma$ is a positive real number that balances the two loss terms in Eq. (9). $\tilde { x } _ { 0 } ~ = ~ \tilde { x } ( t ~ = ~ 0 )$ is the predicted initial condition (i.e., the first element in the PIRNN model output vector $\tilde { x } ( t ) = h ( z ; \theta ) , \forall t \in [ 0 , T ] )$ . Since the generalization error $R _ { D } ( \theta )$ is difficult to compute due to the unknown probability distribution $Z ,$ it is generally approximated by an empirical error calculated using a finite set of training samples $z _ { i }$ drawn from the same distributions Z as follows.

$$
R _ {S} (\theta) := \frac {1}{N _ {\mathcal {G}}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N _ {\mathcal {G}}} L \left(\mathcal {G} (h (z _ {i}; \theta), u), 0\right) + \gamma \frac {1}{N _ {\mathcal {G}}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N _ {\mathcal {G}}} L \left(\tilde {x} _ {0, i}, x _ {0, i}\right)\tag{10}
$$

where $N _ { \mathcal { G } }$ is the number of training samples from the same distribution $Z .$ Note that in Eq. (9), the first term of the generalization error represents the loss term computed based on the first-principles model of Eq. (4) and the second term penalizes the deviation between the predicted initial condition and its actual value. To achieve a desired training performance in the sense that the PIRNN model captures both the dynamics of Eq. (4) and fits the initial condition $x _ { 0 } ,$ the weight parameter $\gamma$ needs to be carefully chosen to balance the two loss terms. Therefore, to mitigate the impact of γ on the overall training performance, and also to simplify the analysis of the PIRNN generalization error by maintaining only one loss term in the loss function, we design a new PIRNN model $\tilde { h } ( z ; \theta )$ that approximates the original PIRNN model $h ( z ; \theta )$

$$
h (z; \theta) = (t - t _ {0}) \tilde {h} (z; \theta) + x _ {0}\tag{11}
$$

It can be observed from Eq. (11) that $h ( z ; \theta ) = x _ { 0 }$ holds when $t = t _ { 0 }$ regardless of the choice of $\tilde { h } ( z ; \theta )$ . In other words, through the design of the new PIRNN model ${ \tilde { h } } ( z ; \theta ) ,$ , the initial condition can be predicted accurately by $h ( z ; \theta )$ for any ${ \tilde { h } } ( z ; \theta ) .$ . Therefore, it remains to show that the approximation of $h ( z ; \theta )$ through Eq. (11) can capture the nonlinear dynamics of Eq. (4). Specifically, by substituting Eq. (11) into Eq. (4), we have the following equation:

$$
\tilde {\mathcal {G}} (\tilde {h} (z; \theta), u) := (t - t _ {0}) \dot {\tilde {h}} (z; \theta) + F ((t - t _ {0}) \tilde {h} (z; \theta) + x _ {0}, u) + \tilde {h} (z; \theta).\tag{12}
$$

As a result, the new PIRNN model $\tilde { h } ( z ; \theta )$ is developed by minimizing the loss function $L ( \tilde { \mathcal { G } } ( \tilde { h } ( z ; \theta ) , u ) , 0 )$ only, while the initial condition can be readily satisfied using Eq. (11). The optimal weight matrices of the new PIRNN model $h ( z ; \theta _ { D } )$ can be obtained as follows.

$$
\theta_ {D} = \arg \min _ {\theta} R _ {D} (\theta) := \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {z} \sim Z} [ L (\tilde {\mathcal {G}} (\tilde {h} (z; \theta), u), 0) ].\tag{13}
$$

Using Eq. (11), the PIRNN model $h ( z ; \theta _ { D } )$ that meets both the initial condition and the physical law of Eq. (4) is given by

$$
h (z; \theta_ {D}) = (t - t _ {0}) \tilde {h} (z; \theta_ {D}) + x _ {0}\tag{14}
$$

Similar to Eq. (10), the optimization of the PIRNN weights can be performed based on the minimization of the empirical error as follows.

$$
\theta_ {S} = \arg \min _ {\theta} R _ {S} (\theta) := \frac {1}{N _ {\mathcal {G}}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N _ {\mathcal {G}}} L \left(\tilde {\mathcal {G}} (\tilde {h} (z; \theta), u), 0\right)\tag{15}
$$

Finally, the PIRNN model that approximates the nonlinear system of $\operatorname { E q . } \left( 1 \mathrm { a } \right)$ based on the training dataset is obtained as $h ( z ; \theta _ { S } ) =$ $( t - t _ { 0 } ) \tilde { h } ( z ; \theta _ { S } ) + x _ { 0 }$ . Therefore, through the design of PIRNN model $h ( z ; \theta _ { D } )$ using Eq. (11), the training process for the original PIRNN model $h ( z ; \theta _ { D } )$ using the loss function of Eq. (9) is equivalent <sub>to</sub> <sub>training</sub> <sub>the</sub> <sub>new</sub> <sub>PIRNN</sub> <sub>model h</sub>˜<sub>(z</sub>; $\theta _ { D } )$ using the new loss function of Eq. (13). Additionally, since the new loss function of Eq. (13) only includes one loss term, and does not depend on the weight parameter $\gamma ,$ , the generalization error analysis developed in Ref. Recursive feasibility and closed-loop stability of the nonlinear system of Eq. (1) under the LMPC of Eq. (22) for all times, (2) the closed-loop state can be driven towards the origin, and ultimately bounded in the terminal set under $\boldsymbol { u } = \boldsymbol { \varPhi } _ { n n } ( \boldsymbol { x } )$ , and (3) due to the two Lyapunov-based constraints of Eqs. (22e)–(22f), the optimal solution solved by LMPC will achieve an equally good closedloop performance, if not better, than the stabilizing controller. Interested readers may refer to Ref. [38] for the detailed proof of closed-loop stability for the nominal system of Eq. (1), and Ref. [44] for the discussion of closed-loop stability for uncertain nonlinear systems with stochastic disturbances.

## 6.1. Process data generation

In continuous flow processes, operational data collected are generally clustered within a small region around the steady-state, and this renders purely data-driven modeling approaches implausible and unattractive since extrapolation beyond the operating range captured by the process data is immensely hampered by its limited and ill-sampled nature. Furthermore, real-world process data garnered are often corrupted by random measurement noise due to instrumentation uncertainties and hardware limitations, and this poses an additional practical barrier for effective learning that is ascribed to the high capacity of neural networks in overfitting the noisy data. Nevertheless, the latter challenge can be adequately addressed by using the dropout technique, which is a powerful and yet facile regularization method that reduces overfitting in neural networks. Functionally, the dropout method randomly masks selected neural network weights during training, thereby deliberately reducing dependencies in deep neural networks. This haphazardly dropping of connections between neurons in a network facilitates the intrinsic reduction of model complexity and the realization of several independent internal representations of the training data which are instrumental in promoting the learning of the underlying true system dynamics. Specifically, standard dropout technique has been successfully applied to various types of neural networks to reduce overfitting, and Monte Carlo (MC) dropout that was proposed by Ref. [45] has been demonstrated to achieve better performance for RNNs with some types of data noise. For example, in Ref. [46], we have integrated MC dropout with purely data-driven RNNs for modeling nonlinear systems with noisy data. It is demonstrated in Ref. [46] that an RNN model using MC dropout method is able to reduce the overfitting to non-Gaussian noisy data. The main difference between MC dropout and standard dropout techniques is that the random masking behavior is preserved after training for MC dropout, but not for standard dropout. Therefore, the predictions made by RNNs with MC dropout are seen as random samples drawn from a probabilistic distribution, which will be non-deterministic. While MC dropout may be superior to standard dropout for some types of data noise (e.g., non-Gaussian noise), we have shown in Ref. [46] that standard NN modeling without dropout, or with standard dropout may achieve a comparable modeling accuracy to that using MC dropout for noisy data of Gaussian distribution. Therefore, in this work, the standard dropout method is used in PIRNN to reduce overfitting to data with Gaussian noise, and is demonstrated to achieve the desired modeling performance in this section.

Additionally, process data are assumed to be limited and are available only in a small region around the unstable steadystate of $( C _ { A _ { s } } , \ T _ { s } ) = ( 1 . 9 5 \ \mathrm { k m o l } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 3 }$ , 402 K). Specifically, similar to Section 6.2.1, the system states are initially set at their respective steady-state values of $( \varDelta C _ { A } , \ \varDelta T ) =$ (0, 0), which are then subject to small perturbations triggered by the pseudo-randomly generated manipulated input profiles (as shown in Fig. 6) in a sample and hold fashion where $\varDelta C _ { A 0 } \sim$ $U ( - 1 . 5 , ~ 1 . 5 )$ and $\Delta Q \sim \mathsf { \bar { U } } ( - 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } , \ 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } )$ are fed to the RNN models as piecewise constant functions for 15 sampling periods from $t = 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ t o ~ } t = 0 . 1 5 \mathrm { ~ }$ h. Subsequently, the dynamic system is subject to more vigorous control actions with $\varDelta C _ { A 0 } \sim$ U(−3.5, 3.5) and $\Delta Q \sim U ( - 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } , ~ 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } )$ for another 15 sampling periods from $t ~ = ~ 0 . 1 5 \mathrm { ~ h ~ t o ~ } t ~ = ~ 0 . 3$ h. Furthermore, while the true process dynamics without measurement noise, subject to the same manipulated input profiles shown in Fig. 6, is used as the benchmark to evaluate the prediction and noise rejection performance of the three RNN models, the system states that are supplied to the RNN models are corrupted by the measurement noise $\omega _ { C _ { A } }$ and $\omega _ { T }$ , respectively.

As shown in Fig. 5, it is evident that both the purely datadriven RNN (trained using solely noisy process data) and PIRNN (trained using noisy process data and collocation points) are robust against measurement noise where their predictions conform more closely to the true underlying process dynamics between the time interval $t = 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ t o ~ } t = 0 . 1 5 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ h. This can be ascribed to the inclusion of dropout layers that aid in noise rejection by preventing overfitting via model complexity reduction. Moreover, as the observed dynamics of the system states $( \mathrm { i } . { \mathsf { e } } . , \varDelta C _ { A }$ and ∆T profiles shown in Fig. 5) and the manipulated inputs (i.e., ∆C and ∆Q profiles shown in Fig. 6) are found to remain relatively close to their respective steady-state values from $t ~ = ~ 0$ h to $t ~ = ~ 0 . 1 5$ h, their dynamic behaviors have already been well captured by the purely data-driven RNN and PIRNN models in their training phases. It is also noticed that, in Fig. 5, the noise rejection performance of the purely physics-driven RNN model is inferior to that of the RNN and PIRNN models, and this is expected since the noisy process data has been excluded from training the purely physics-driven RNN model, and its adaptability to measurement noise is therefore unsatisfactory.

However, as the dynamic system experiences more rigorous manipulated inputs of larger magnitudes beyond $t = 0 . 1 5 \ \mathrm { h } ,$ the system states begin to drift considerably from their respectively steady-state values, and the prediction performance of the purely data-driven RNN model becomes unsatisfactory, as shown by the appreciable deviations of its predicted state profiles from the ground truth in Fig. 5 from $t = 0 . 2$ h onward. This highlights the limitation of the purely data-driven RNN model, which suffers from poor generalizability beyond the range of training data. In contrast, the PIRNN and the purely physics-driven RNN models, which have been incorporated with the knowledge of the underlying process dynamics through the governing physical laws of Eq. (23), demonstrate excellent extrapolation performance where their predicted state profiles are well matched with the true profiles, as shown in Fig. 5 from $t ~ = ~ 0 . 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } \mathrm { t } 0 \mathrm { ~ } t ~ = ~ 0 . 3$ h. The advantage of physics-informed learning over conventional algorithms manifests in its integration of a priori knowledge with existing data in augmenting the generalization capability of the resulting RNN models, and this is especially instrumental for controlling dynamic systems in the chemical industry, where the mechanistic knowledge (e.g., the governing ODEs of the system) can be directly embedded into the RNN models to enhance their predictive performance in MPC. Note that beyond $t ~ = ~ 0 . 1 5 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ , inputs to all the three RNN models switched from noisy states to true system states as both the PIRNN and the purely physicsdriven RNN are trained to approximate the noise-free dynamic model of Eq. (23) based on the collocation points. In addition, it is noticed that the overall prediction performance by the PIRNN and purely physics-driven RNN are inferior to those seen in Fig. 3 for $t \geq 0 . 1 5 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ . This can be attributed to the inclusion of dropout layers, which reduces the model complexity and hence their ability to model the complex nonlinear dynamics.

Remark 6. As demonstrated in Fig. 7, the true process dynamic model- and the purely physics-driven RNN-based LMPCs are capable of providing adequate control to successfully drive the CSTR from $x _ { 0 }$ to a small neighborhood around its steadystate setpoints, and the effect of measurement noise on the two LMPCs is minor in the initial operation of the CSTR as the absolute difference between $x _ { 0 }$ and the setpoint is much larger in magnitude than that of the Gaussian noise. Therefore, the prediction performances of the true process dynamic model and the purely physics-driven RNN will only become inferior to PIRNN when the predicted states required are close to the input state x supplied, and the adverse impact of measurement noise thus manifests only when the LMPCs are required to stabilize the system in a small neighborhood $( \mathbf { e } . \mathbf { g } . , \varOmega _ { \rho _ { m i n } } )$ around its steady-state setpoints.

Remark 7. While more sophisticated RNN variants (such as long short-term memory (LSTM) and gated recurrent unit (GRU) networks) exist, they tend to have minimal impacts on the model generalizability since all of them are data-driven models in nature. Additionally, the dominant benefits of LSTM and GRU over traditional RNN, which include a better capacity at handling long sequential data as they are less susceptible to the vanishing gradient problem [47], are not explicitly favored in this case as the number of time steps required in the model prediction is only 10. In fact, the exploitation of more involved RNN architectures such as LSTM and GRU might even be detrimental to LMPC implementations as these complex networks generally require more plant data in order to learn effectively. Moreover, the training of LSTM and GRU can be computationally intensive given their inherent more complicated architecture, and this may engender a potential hindrance to their applications in real-time online learning MPC, where the predictive performance of RNN models are improved in real-time by updating them using the latest plant data retrieved once a significant discrepancy between model predictions and sensor measurements are discovered. Refs. [2,48] demonstrate the outstanding modeling capacity of RNN in describing a highly complex and nonlinear batch crystallization process, and its superior computational efficiency in an online learning-based RNN-MPC scheme. In principle, although the physics-informed modeling approach can be applied to LSTM and GRU, the number of prediction time steps required should be sufficiently large that renders the simple RNN ineffective, and a switch to the more complex variants is thus necessary to warrant adequate modeling performance for satisfactory control. In other cases, RNN should be the preferred choice of surrogate model in LMPC applications.

## 7. Conclusion

In this work, we developed a physics-informed recurrent neural network modeling scheme that seamlessly integrates the noisy process data and mechanistic mathematical models in augmenting the learning process. A generalization error bound was first derived for the PIRNN models based on the nominal system using the Rademacher complexity technique from statistical learning theory. Subsequently, a PIRNN-LMPC control scheme was formulated and implemented to control nonlinear systems subject to Gaussian measurement noise. A chemical reactor example was employed to demonstrate the effectiveness of the PIRNN-LMPC scheme, in which the dropout technique was utilized to facilitate the learning of the underlying process dynamics (ground truth) and to prevent overfitting the noisy data. Both the open- and closed-loop simulations demonstrated improved model accuracy and control performance using PIRNN models.
