# 1. Introduction

A refinery is instrumented to within an inch of its life, and yet the moment that decides whether an upset becomes an incident is still measured in minutes. Catch a developing fault early and an operator can contain it; miss it for a quarter of an hour and the same disturbance can cascade into an unplanned shutdown, off-spec product, or a hazard to people and the environment. Automated fault detection and diagnosis (FDD) sits at the centre of abnormal-situation management for exactly this reason, and the data-driven branch of FDD has matured quickly as plants have accumulated years of historized sensor data (Ji and Sun, 2022).

The classical foundation of the field is multivariate statistical process monitoring built on principal component analysis and partial least squares. These methods are interpretable, cheap, and still competitive as a first line of defence (Qin, 2003; Ji and Sun, 2022). Over the past decade deep learning has pushed reported accuracy well beyond them: convolutional networks that reshape windowed sensor data into image-like tensors (Wu and Zhao, 2018), extended deep belief networks that preserve raw-signal information across layers (Wang et al., 2019), topology-aware graph networks that inject process connectivity (Wu et al., 2021), and temporal architectures with generative augmentation for the hardest faults (Lomov et al., 2021). A parallel line of work predicts hard-to-measure quality variables from routine measurements through soft sensing (Sun and Ge, 2021; Yuan et al., 2020; Sifakis et al., 2023).

Impressive as these numbers are, three assumptions stand between them and a deployment.

The first is that **evaluation happens on balanced, simulated data.** The Tennessee Eastman splits that dominate the literature are balanced by construction, whereas a running plant produces dangerous faults rarely, and incipient faults with a low signal-to-noise ratio are precisely the ones a classifier tends to miss. A model that scores well on a balanced test can still be blind to the events that matter most.

The second is the **closed-world assumption.** A classifier trained on a fixed catalogue of faults will confidently assign any novel disturbance to one of its known labels. In a control room a misfiled unknown fault is worse than one flagged as "I don't recognise this," because the wrong label invites the wrong response. The machine-learning community has formalized this as open-set recognition and out-of-distribution detection (Yang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024), but the process-monitoring literature rarely questions the closed set.

The third is **overconfidence.** Softmax probabilities are systematically inflated and are poor indicators of whether an input resembles the training data at all (Sensoy et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2020). An operator given a bare class label, with no trustworthy sense of when to distrust it, cannot use the model to make a safety decision.

Cutting across all three is the gap between simulator and plant. Models are validated on simulators, but distribution shift and non-stationary operation degrade them on real equipment (Hinder et al., 2023; Lobo et al., 2023), and a simulator and a real facility rarely even share the same sensors.

This paper treats the three assumptions as one problem. We deliberately avoid the common pattern of bolting a separate model onto each concern (one network for classification, a random forest for novelty, a statistical monitor for detection), because a framework assembled from unrelated parts is not a framework. Instead we commit to a single convolutional backbone and show that it can carry every task: diagnosis, incipient-fault detection, unknown-fault rejection at a controlled false-alarm rate, and interpretable attribution. The contributions are:

1. **A single-backbone, trustworthy FDD framework** in which imbalance handling, calibrated detection, open-set rejection, and interpretability are all computed from the same network, evaluated under the canonical Tennessee Eastman train/test protocol rather than a re-split of the training simulations.
2. **A calibrated operating point.** Rather than reporting an arbitrary argmax rule, we fix the decision threshold on validation data to a stated false-alarm budget, and report fault-detection rate, false-alarm rate, and detection delay at that point, the numbers a plant actually cares about.
3. **Evidence that the backbone is not benchmark-specific**, by training the identical network on real multiphase-flow pilot-plant data, plus auxiliary evidence on a batch bioprocess, a refinery soft-sensor benchmark, and a metallurgical fault set.
4. **An honest account of what does not work**, including a negative sim-to-real transfer result, reported because it is more useful to the field than a flattering but misleading alternative.
