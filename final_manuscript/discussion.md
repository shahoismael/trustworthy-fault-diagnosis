# 5. Discussion

## 5.1 Trustworthiness, not another accuracy point
The Tennessee Eastman leaderboard is crowded, and chasing a higher closed-set accuracy would add little. Our results argue that the useful frontier lies elsewhere: in whether a model knows when to raise an alarm, when to abstain, and why. The three headline results speak to this directly. A calibrated operating point (FDR 0.90 at FAR 0.06) turns a classifier into a monitor an operator can live with. A feature-space rejection score (AUROC 0.82) lets the model flag faults it was never trained on. Physically coherent attribution lets an engineer sanity-check an alarm. None of these is a new algorithm in isolation; the contribution is that all of them come from one network and are reported under a deployment-oriented protocol.

## 5.2 What the numbers mean for process safety
The metrics in this paper map directly onto the two failure modes that matter in a control room. A missed or late detection is the path from an abnormal situation to an incident: the same disturbance that an operator can contain in the first minutes can, if unseen, escalate into an unplanned trip, off-spec release, or a hazard to personnel and the environment. Our mean detection delay of 47.8 minutes on Tennessee Eastman, at a fixed 0.90 detection rate, is the quantity that bounds how much reaction time the operator is left with; shortening it is worth more than a marginal gain in classification accuracy. A false alarm is the opposite failure: nuisance trips and alarm floods desensitize operators and are a recognized contributor to poor abnormal-situation management. This is why we fix the operating point to a stated false-alarm budget rather than reporting argmax accuracy, and why the drop from a roughly 0.9 to a 0.06 false-alarm rate is, for a safety application, the single most consequential result in the paper. Finally, the open-set component addresses the most dangerous class of event for a data-driven monitor: a genuinely novel fault that a closed-set classifier would silently misfile as a known, benign condition. Flagging such an input as unrecognized, rather than mislabelling it, is precisely the behaviour a safety case requires.

## 5.3 The false-alarm result is the most practically important
The gap between a naïve argmax rule and a validation-calibrated threshold, false-alarm rates of roughly 0.9 versus 0.06 at comparable detection, explains why many high-accuracy models never leave the bench. Multi-class classifiers optimized for macro-F1 will happily trade normal-class precision for fault recall, which is exactly the wrong trade in a plant where nuisance alarms erode operator trust. Fixing the operating point on validation data is simple, but it is the step that makes the numbers meaningful to a process engineer, and we would encourage the field to report FAR at a stated budget rather than argmax accuracy.

## 5.4 Why softmax fails and distance succeeds for unknown faults
That maximum-softmax, entropy and energy scores sat near chance while the Mahalanobis distance reached 0.82 is consistent with the broader out-of-distribution literature (Lee et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2024): a network trained to separate known classes does not, as a by-product, learn calibrated confidence about inputs unlike anything it has seen. The penultimate feature geometry carries that information; the output probabilities largely do not. For practitioners the takeaway is concrete: build the rejection rule in feature space, not on the softmax.

## 5.5 The focal objective and the limits of the incipient-fault gain
The ablation is clear that the focal loss, not class weighting, recovers faults 3, 9 and 15. It is equally clear that the gain is uneven and seed-sensitive: fault 3 is reliably recovered, fault 15 much less so, and recall varies across random initializations. This is not a defect of the method so much as a property of the faults, whose signal-to-noise ratio is low by construction, and we report the variability rather than selecting a favourable seed. Closing this gap fully likely requires either active excitation of the process or architectural changes beyond a fixed-window CNN.

## 5.6 Generalization and the honest limit of transfer
Training the same architecture on real PRONTO data (AUROC 0.99) is strong evidence that the approach is not tied to a single simulator. It is important to be precise about what this does and does not show. It shows the *architecture* generalizes to a real multiphase process when trained on that process. It does **not** show that a model trained on the simulator transfers to the plant; our transfer experiment fails (AUROC 0.44–0.46) because the simulated and real facilities share no sensor space. We regard reporting this negative result as part of the paper's contribution. The community would benefit from cross-domain FDD benchmarks with a shared representation, which are currently scarce.

## 5.7 Threats to validity
Three deserve emphasis. First, although the Tennessee Eastman classification head is now reported over five seeds on the canonical test set (macro-F1 0.88 ± 0.06), the incipient-fault recall within that average is highly seed-sensitive, so the recovery of faults 3 and 9 is better read as a distribution than as a point value. Second, the unknown-fault set is a single held-out triple (16, 17, 18); rotating the unknown set over all faults would give a more complete open-set picture. Third, the IndPenSim result is sample-level and near-trivial, and the auxiliary datasets use task-appropriate models rather than the shared backbone; they are generalization evidence, not part of the unified-framework claim. None of these undermines the central results, but each is a fair target for a reviewer and is stated plainly in Section 5.8.

## 5.8 Limitations
- **Sim-to-real transfer is unsolved here.** With no shared sensor space between the simulator and the real plant, naïve transfer is at or below chance; the positive real-plant result is same-architecture, target-trained.
- **Incipient-fault recall is variable across seeds**, reflecting the inherent difficulty of low-signal faults rather than instability of the training procedure.
- **Open-set evaluation uses one held-out unknown set**; it is not rotated over all faults.
- **Uncertainty is delivered by a feature-space distance, not by an evidential head**, which underperformed in our experiments and is therefore not the headline mechanism.
- **Auxiliary datasets are evaluated with task-appropriate models**, and the IndPenSim detection is sample-level, so these support, rather than constitute, the unified-framework claim.
