# Imbalanced Chemical Process Fault Diagnosis Using Balancing GAN With Active Sample Selection

Peng Peng , Hanrong Zhang , Graduate Student Member, IEEE, Xinyue Wang, Graduate Student Member, IEEE, Wanqiu Huang , and Hongwei Wang , Member, IEEE

Abstract—Current deep-learning-based fault diagnosis methods, though proven to be successful with sufficient fault data, cannot well address the challenges of sample availability in real-world industrial scenarios. To address this challenge, this article proposes an effective approach by exploiting data generation and sample selection techniques. Specifically, we first develop a balancing generative adversarial network (BAGAN)-based data generation technique to generate more discriminative fault samples by utilizing not only the fault samples, but also the normal samples. Second, a strategy is devised to select the samples generated by BAGAN, and on this basis, active learning (AL) is utilized to select the most informative samples. The stacked autoencoder (SAE)-based deep neural network (DNN) is used to classify the faults. The proposed method is evaluated by conducting computational experiments on the Tennessee Eastman (TE) dataset. The evaluation results demonstrate that the proposed BAGAN-based method with an active sample selection strategy achieves improved performance in imbalanced chemical fault diagnosis tasks.

Among the methods developed for monitoring industrial processes, the data-driven methods have become continuously more prevailing including Fisher discriminant analysis (FDA) [1], support vector machines (SVMs) [2], neural networks (NNs) [3], and so on [4]. However, these methods mostly rely upon sufficient training data to obtain promising results. In most real-world industrial scenarios, data samples are very limited, and, as such, the need of researching advanced methods for this case has been raised.

Researchers have started exploring solutions for this problem and the generative adversarial network (GAN)-based imbalanced fault diagnosis methods have attracted substantial attention. For instance, Zhou et al. [5] design the new generator and discriminator of GAN to generate more discriminative fault samples using a scheme of global optimization. Li et al. [6] proposed a novel cross-domain fault diagnosis method using deep generative NNs to tackle scenarios where there is a lack of adequate testing data for training purposes. Wang et al. [7] employed a Wasserstein GAN (WGAN) to produce simulated mechanical signals, utilizing a labeled dataset as the basis for their work. Nevertheless, there are two following drawbacks in the most existing GAN-based fault diagnosis methods: 1) the generation process of GAN does not utilize the label information and only a few fault samples are used during the training phase and 2) adding synthetic samples to the training set is a “blind process” which cannot ensure improved classification performance.

For the first drawback, the generated samples by GAN can be easily confused with other normal/fault classes as GAN only consider one fault at a time [8]. In this sense, the generated samples may be not reliable to improve the classification performance. Till now, some works have attempted to utilize conditional information to improve the performance of GANbased fault diagnosis methods. For example, Yang et al. [9] developed the conditional version of GANs (CGANs) to generate fault samples conditioned on class labels. Meng et al. [10] introduced an improved conditional synthesis model, that is, the auxiliary classifier GAN (ACGAN), to produce highquality fault samples by allowing the discriminator to classify the samples from different classes.

For the second drawback, almost all of the previous studies choose to blindly add synthetic samples to the original data and few consider how to ensure the quality of synthetic samples in this field. We can sample an infinite number of synthetic samples from a trained GAN model as the number of inputs from the Gaussian distribution is infinite. However, not all the generated samples will improve the performance of classification models, and even some generated samples may degrade the performance of classification models. Therefore, some researchers have started to focus on selecting synthetic samples in other fields. For example, in the medical field, Xue et al. [11] selected the generated samples based on the confidence of their assigned labels and their feature similarity to real labeled images to improve the performance on a medical histopathology dataset.

The imbalanced fault diagnosis methods based on GAN may encounter the above-mentioned difficulties. In this article, we propose balancing GAN (BAGAN)-based data-generating techniques to conquer these difficulties. BAGAN utilizes conditional information in the training phase to generate multiple minority-class samples simultaneously [12]. This is a departure from existing methods that rely solely on the generative model to generate minority class samples. By incorporating conditional information, the BAGAN can generate more discriminative samples that better capture the characteristics of the minority class, leading to better diagnosis performance. Besides, a sample selection strategy is devised to select the generated samples and active learning (AL) is utilized to single out the most informative ones. This is in contrast to existing methods that typically rely on random sampling to select samples. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to use BAGAN with particular consideration of selecting the generated fault samples in the field of fault diagnosis.

The main contributions of this article are summarized as follows.

1) The BAGAN is introduced to generate the faults samples as a few minority classes, which may not be enough to train a GAN with satisfactory performance. The conditional information is utilized in the training phase, so BAGAN can generate multiple minority class samples at once. As such, it is easier for BAGAN to generate more discriminative samples.

2) A sample selection strategy is also designed to restore balance in imbalanced datasets. The quality of generated samples may be either good or bad. High-quality samples need to be selected, while low-quality samples need to be discarded. In this article, an AL-based sample selection strategy is developed to make selections in the generated samples. The selected samples are guaranteed to help improve diagnosis performance.

3) Comprehensive experiments are carried out on the Tennessee Eastman (TE) dataset. We verify our proposed method in both binary and multiclass imbalanced fault diagnosis tasks. And a detailed ablation study is carried out to illustrate the effectiveness of the BAGAN and AL-based sample selecting strategy.

The rest of this article is organized as follows. The details of BAGAN are described in Section II. In Section III, the proposed imbalanced chemical process fault diagnosis using the BAGAN-based framework is described detailedly. In Section IV, we conduct extensive experiments on the TE process dataset to verify the effectiveness of the proposed framework. The main conclusions of this work are summarized in Section V.

## II. BACKGROUND THEORY

## A. From GAN to BAGAN

GAN is a popular generative model and the basic idea of GAN is to train a generator in an adversarial mode against a discriminator [13]. The objective function can be formulated as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \underset {G} {\operatorname * {m i n m a x}} _ {D} V (D, G) \\ = E _ {x \sim p _ {\mathrm{data}} (x)} [ \log D (x) ] + E _ {z \sim p _ {z} (z)} [ \log (1 - D (G (z))) ] \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where z is a random vector used as the input of generator G. Generator G targets to generate fake samples G(z) with the same distribution as the true data X and thus fool the discriminator D to the greatest extent. D(x) denotes the probability that x comes from the real data. The discriminator D seeks to maximize the probability of assigning correct labels to both training examples and the samples from G. The goal of generator G is to minimize log(1 − D(G(z))).

It is noted that GAN is not specifically developed for imbalanced problems and turns out to be flawed when targeting the generation of minority-class images. So, the BAGAN is proposed to address this issue [12]. BAGAN is developed from ACGAN [14]. For ACGAN, every sample generated has a corresponding class label c in addition to noise z. The objective function of ACGAN consists of two parts: $L _ { S }$ and $L _ { C }$ $L _ { S }$ represents the log-likelihood of the correct source and $L _ { C }$ represents the log-likelihood of the correct class, which are listed as follows:

$$
L _ {S} = E \left[ \log P (S = \text { real } | X _ {\text { real }}) \right] + E \left[ \log P (S = \text { fake } | X _ {\text { fake }}) \right]\tag{2}
$$

$$
L _ {C} = E \left[ \log P (C = c | X _ {\mathrm{real}}) \right] + E \left[ \log P (C = c | X _ {\mathrm{fake}}) \right]\tag{3}
$$

the discriminator tries to maximize $L _ { S } + L _ { C }$ , while the generator is trained to maximize $L _ { S } - L _ { C }$

Fig. 1. Illustration of BAGAN.

The detailed structure of BAGAN is shown in Fig. 1. The generator and the discriminator of BAGAN are conditioned on class labels, which allows the generator to create samples for specific classes, enabling the generation of multiple minority class samples simultaneously. BAGAN incorporates information from both the majority and minority classes during the training process. It uses autoencoders to learn a shared latent space representation for both the majority and minority classes. This shared latent space allows BAGAN to leverage the structure and patterns of the majority class samples while generating new minority class samples. After training, BAGAN generates synthetic minority class samples to balance the imbalanced dataset. Slightly different from ACGAN, the BAGAN discriminator has a single output that returns either a problem-specific class label c or the label fake. More details about the training phase of BAGAN can refer to Mariani et al.’s [12] work.

## B. Evaluation Index

In this article, G-means will be adopted for comparing our methods with the existing state-of-the-art methods. For imbalanced classification problems, G-means is defined as

$$
G \text {-means} = \sqrt {\frac {\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}} \times \frac {\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TN} + \mathrm{FP}}}\tag{4}
$$

where TP is the true positive, TN is the true negative, FP is the false positive, and FN is the false negative.

## III. PROPOSED ALBAGAN-BASED IMBALANCED FAULT DIAGNOSIS FRAMEWORK

In this article, we propose a BAGAN-based data-generating technique. This technique, combined with the sample selection strategy, aims to solve the imbalanced process fault diagnosis problems. To explain the framework in detail, the training of BAGAN is described first. Then the training of stacked autoencoder (SAE)-based deep NN (DNN) is briefly investigated. On this basis, AL-based selective synthetic augmentation is designed. Finally, the proposed sample selection using activelearning-based BAGAN (ALBAGAN) is formulated.

The training of BAGAN is similar to traditional GAN. The difference is that BAGAN is initialized with a trained autoencoder to avoid mode collapse. After the BAGAN is initialized, the generator and the discriminator are trained iteratively. The whole training process is shown in Algorithm 1.

The training process of the SAE-based DNN can be referred to in Bengio et al.’s [15] work. In short, it is mainly comprised of three steps. Primarily, the DNN is trained by an unsupervised layer-by-layer greedy training algorithm. After getting the weights of hidden layers, the top layer (softmax layer) is trained. Finally, all the layers are fine-tuned.

Based on the two algorithms described above, we propose an AL-based sample selection strategy. AL aims to select the instances which can help increase the performance of the machine learning model [16], [17]. And as such, we naturally adopt AL to select the generated samples. The details are as follows. Initially, the original dataset is utilized for training the DNN and BAGAN. Subsequently, the trained DNN is employed to choose the samples produced by BAGAN. In this stage, any instances that are generated and cause confusion with other classes will be eliminated. The reason is that the well-trained DNN will accurately categorize the top-quality generated examples. Among the chosen examples, we learn from the underlying principles of AL to select the most informative ones, which are deemed more significant than the other artificially generated examples. In this article, we adopt the classification margin as the criterion of selection [18]

$$
M (X) = P (\hat {X} _ {1} | X) - P (\hat {X} _ {2} | X)\tag{5}
$$

where ${ \hat { X } } _ { 1 }$ and ${ \hat { X } } _ { 2 }$ are the first and second most likely classes. When the margin of a sample is less than a preset threshold, it will be considered the informative one. The value of the threshold is set as 0.1 in our article. The whole selection strategy is shown in Algorithm 2.

## IV. EXPERIMENT

In this section, the TE dataset is adopted to evaluate the performance of our proposed method [19]. TE is a dataset obtained by running a simulation using actual data from a chemical process and as such the experiments on this dataset are much more convincing.

## A. Dataset Description

The data can be obtained from the website: http://depts. washington.edu/control/LARRY/TE/download.html. There are in total 21 faults in the TE dataset. In the training data, there are 500 samples for the normal condition and there are 480 samples for each fault. There are 160 normal samples and

800 fault samples for each fault’s testing data. All the samples are utilized in the balanced circumstance, while some of them are randomly selected by the unbalanced ratio for imbalanced fault diagnosis.

## B. Experimental Setup

We implement the experiments mainly based on the Python libraries Scikit-Learn and Keras [20]. In the experiments, we compare the proposed method with the other five state-ofthe-art methods, namely DNN, GAN [13], WGAN-GP [21], CGAN [22], and ACGAN-DNN [23]. Specifically, the DNN is used to show the effectiveness of GAN-based oversampling techniques; and the GAN, WGAN-GP, CGAN, and ACGAN are compared to our method to show the advantages of BAGAN. We employed a grid search approach to select the optimal hyperparameters. The detailed parameters of the networks are shown in Table I. As GAN, WGAN-GP, CGAN, ACGAN, and BAGAN share the same discriminator and generator, we use the GANs to represent them. Adam is used as the optimizer [24].

## C. Results Analysis

Both binary imbalanced fault diagnosis and multiclass imbalanced fault diagnosis are explored in our experiments. For binary fault diagnosis, we set the unbalanced ratio as 10:1 and the comparison results are shown in Table II. It can be seen that the ALBAGAN-based methods achieve the best performance compared to other methods. ## D. Analysis of Data Selection Process

For binary fault diagnosis, we choose Fault 5 as an example and visualize the generating and selecting process. Specifically, t-SNE is adopted to reduce the feature dimensions (input of DNN’s fully connected layer) to 2, which are then plotted by the class afterward. In Figs. 3(a) and 4(a), the generated samples by GAN and BAGAN are shown. It should be noted that BAGAN can generate more discriminative samples. As GAN only considers the fault samples in the training stage, some generated samples may inevitably be similar to the normal samples. So, we can observe that some fault samples generated by GAN are close to the normal samples. Since BAGAN considers both normal samples and fault samples, it can generate more discriminative samples. These generated fault samples have shown similar characteristics to real fault samples. For both GAN and BAGAN, it is necessary to select the generated samples. The selection process is shown in Figs. 3(b) and 4(b). Some generated samples are discarded as they are closer to the normal samples. For the selected generated samples, the most informative ones selected by AL are the closest to the classification plane. In Figs. 3(c) and 4(c), we add the test data further, which is utilized to verify the effectiveness of the generated samples. For example, as shown in the top right section of Fig. 4(c), few fault samples of the training set (depicted in orange) cover the fault samples in the test set (depicted in purple). In contrast, the selected generation samples (depicted in green) compensate for the missing portion. In other words, combining the fault training samples with the selected generation samples enhances the overall effectiveness of the training process.

For multiclass fault diagnosis, the selected samples by ALBAGAN under the unbalanced ratio of 10:1 are shown in Fig. 5. As the discriminator of BAGAN tries to classify the generated samples, it can produce more discriminative samples. In Fig. 5, we can see that the generated fault samples overlap with the corresponding category. It is noted that some samples of Fault 14 are behind the selected generative samples and the selected generation samples of Fault 14 cover some of the Fault 14 samples. And the generated samples do not overlap between the classes. Therefore, the generated are not just copies of the actual samples, but also come from the real distribution of the corresponding fault class, which can help increase the diagnosis model performance indeed.

## E. Ablation Study

As shown in Table IV, we also conduct the ablation experiment to verify the effectiveness of the BAGAN and AL-based data selection strategy. Model A denotes that GAN is used to generate the fault samples and no data selection strategy is utilized. It gets an average G-means of 73.39% and 96.95%, respectively. If the BAGAN (Model B) or the AL-based data selection strategy (Model C) is used, the average G-means will increase. For example, Model B is increased by 1.19% for binary imbalanced fault diagnosis and 0.63% for multiclass imbalanced fault diagnosis, compared to Model A. And if both BAGAN and AL-based data selection strategies are used, the proposed method gets the highest average G-means. These results prove the importance of introducing BAGAN and ALbased data selection strategy.

## V. CONCLUSION

In this article, a BAGAN-based data-generating technique is proposed for imbalanced fault diagnosis. Apart from incorporating a novel AL-based data selection strategy, the technique also utilizes the sample-sensitive SAE-based DNN to classify faults. BAGAN is first used to restore balance in the ordinary dataset. Then a sample selection strategy is developed to discard the low-quality samples. Finally, the samplesensitive SAE-based DNN is utilized to classify faults.

The ALBAGAN-DNN framework is compared to the existing methods in numerous simulation experiments on the TE dataset. As illustrated in these experiments, our proposed method achieves a considerable enhancement in terms of both the computation process and the quality of results. It is worth noting that the proposed AL-based sample selection strategy can also be utilized to improve the performance of GANsbased methods, which means that the proposed method would be appropriate for a wide range of application scenarios. In our future work, we will specifically focus on the following issues. To begin with, diagnosing faults can be exceedingly difficult when there are only a few samples to work with. To tackle this issue in practical scenarios, we will employ the few-shot learning approach. Additionally, we will concentrate on creating algorithms that can pinpoint the underlying cause of faults, as current deep-learning methods do not provide sufficiently satisfactory results for effective interpretation. Moreover, it is also meant to take the out-of-distribution (OOD) problem into account, which can ensure the success of a model when it is presented with the data distribution of inputs that significantly differ from that of training data [25], [26]. Last, but not least, we have conducted comprehensive experiments on the chemical processes using our proposed approach. In future work, it is also potentially applicable to other process fault diagnoses using tabular data. As far as we know, the difference between different fault diagnosis processes lies in the different data preprocessing, while the algorithms can actually be universal, which means that our proposed method could be extended to other fault diagnosis tasks, such as machinery fault diagnosis, after corresponding data preprocessing.
